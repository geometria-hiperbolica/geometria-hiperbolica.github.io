Linear Fractional Transformations

In 1855, the German mathematician August Ferdinand Möbius (1790-1868) published his book titled Die Theorie der Kreisverwandschaft in rein geometrischer Darstellung (Möbius 1855), in which he developed the theory of circular transformations of the plane. These transformations consist of those that are generated by inversions with respect to circles (to enrich this historical information we recommend the reader consult Kolmogorov & Yushkevich p. 107). From the analytic point of view, these transformations can be interpreted as linear fractional transformations. Hence the reason for using Möbius transformations to name linear fractional transformations.

A linear fractional transformation or Möbius transformation is a function $T$ from the Riemann sphere $\widehat{\mathbb{C}}$ onto itself, whose assignment rule is of the form: \begin{equation}\label{eq:trans_fracc_lineal} T(z)=\frac{az+b}{cz+d}, \end{equation} where the coefficients of the transformation $a,b,c$ and $d$ are complex numbers, such that the determinant of the transformation satisfies the relation ${\rm det}(T)=ad-cb\neq 0.$

Regarding the assignment rule of the transformation $T$ (see equation \eqref{eq:trans_fracc_lineal}) the following considerations are established:

  1. If the coefficient $c=0,$ then the correspondence rule of $T$ is defined by
    \[ T(z)= \left\{ \begin{array}{ cl } \dfrac{az+b}{d}; & \text{ for each } z\in\mathbb{C}, \\ \infty; & \text{ if } z=\infty.\\ \end{array} \right. \]
  2. Conversely, if the coefficient $c\neq 0,$ then the correspondence rule of $T$ is defined by
    \[T(z)= \left\{ \begin{array}{ cl } \dfrac{az+b}{cz+d}; & \text{ for each } z\in\mathbb{C}-\left\{\dfrac{-d}{c}\right\}, \\ \infty; & \text{ if } z=\dfrac{-d}{c},\\ \dfrac{a}{c}; & \text{ if } z=\infty.\\ \end{array} \right. \]

Note that each Möbius transformation $T$ is a continuous bijection of the Riemann sphere $\widehat{\mathbb{C}}$ onto the Riemann sphere $\widehat{\mathbb{C}},$ with continuous inverse. More precisely, for the Möbius transformation $T$ whose assignment rule is described in equation \eqref{eq:trans_fracc_lineal}, its inverse $T^{-1}$ is also a Möbius transformation and, its corresponding assignment rule is given by \[ T^{-1}(z)=\dfrac{\dfrac{d}{k}z-\dfrac{b}{k}}{-\dfrac{c}{k}z+\dfrac{a}{k}}, \] where $k=\sqrt{\det(T)}.$ On the other hand, the assignment rule of the identity transformation ${\rm Id}:\widehat{\mathbb{C}}\to\widehat{\mathbb{C}}$ can be written as \[ {\rm Id}(z)=\frac{1\, z+0}{0\, z+1}, \] that is, ${\rm Id}$ is also a Möbius transformation. The above implies that the set of all Möbius transformations

\begin{equation}\label{eq:grupo_transformaciones_de_Mobius_notacion} \mathcal{M}:=\left\{T:\widehat{\mathbb{C}}\to \widehat{\mathbb{C}}\mid T \text{ is a Möbius transformation}\right\}, \end{equation}
is a subgroup of the homeomorphisms of the Riemann sphere ${\rm Homeo}(\widehat{\mathbb{C}}).$

Let us introduce four canonical Möbius transformations.

  1. The translation function with respect to the complex number $b\in \mathbb{C}^{\ast},$ is that Möbius transformation whose assignment rule is given by \[ T_b(z)=z+b, \] Its inverse is given by $T_b^{-1}(z)=z-b.$
  2. The dilation function with respect to the positive real number $r>0,$ is the Möbius transformation with assignment rule \[ H_r(z)=rz. \] Its corresponding inverse transformation is given by $H_r^{-1}(z)=\dfrac{z}{r}.$
  3. The rotation function with respect to the angle $\theta\in (0, 2\pi),$ is the Möbius transformation with assignment rule \[ R_{\theta}(z)=e^{i\theta}z. \] Its corresponding inverse transformation is given by $R_{-\theta}(z)=e^{-i\theta}z.$
  4. The Möbius transformation whose assignment rule is given by \[ T(z)=\frac{1}{z}, \] is called inversion. Note that inversion is an involution, that is, $T\circ T=Id.$ Moreover, the inversion $T$ leaves invariant the unit circle $\mathbb{S}^{1}:=\{z\in\mathbb{C}: |z|=1\}.$ Also, it sends the set $\{z\in\mathbb{C}:|z|\lt 1\}$ to the set $\{z\in\mathbb{C}:|z|>1\}\cup\{\infty\}.$ Conversely, the inversion $T$ maps the set $\{z\in\mathbb{C}:|z|>1\}\cup\{\infty\}$ onto the set $\{z\in\mathbb{C}:|z|\lt 1\}.$

Using some of the canonical transformations we can convince ourselves that the group $\mathcal{M}$ is not commutative. It is enough to consider the transformations given by the assignment rules \[ T_1(z)=z+1 \quad \text{ and } \quad T_2(z)=\dfrac{-1}{z}, \] then the compositions

\begin{equation*} \begin{split} T_1 \circ T_2(z)&=T_1\left(\dfrac{-1}{z}\right)=\dfrac{-1}{z}+1=\dfrac{z-1}{z},\\ &\\ T_2\circ T_1(z) & =T_2(z+1)=\dfrac{-1}{z+1},\\ \end{split} \end{equation*}
satisfy $T_1 \circ T_2(z)\neq T_2 \circ T_1 (z)$ for some $z\in\widehat{\mathbb{C}}.$

Geometrically, a Möbius transformation can be obtained by means of a stereographic projection of the complex plane to an admissible sphere in $\R^3,$ followed by a rigid motion of the sphere in $\R^3$ (which maps to another admissible sphere), followed by a stereographic projection back to the plane. Identical transformations

From a one-variable Calculus course we know that the real-valued functions $f$ and $g$ given by \[ f(x)=2x^2 \quad \text{ and } \quad g(x)=\frac{4x^{2}}{2}, \] are the "same function", except that their assignment rules are different. For Möbius transformations we have the same phenomenon. Let us introduce a simple definition that allows us to identify when two or more Möbius transformations are identical.

The Möbius transformations $T_1$ and $T_2$ are identical, if and only if, $T_1(z)=T_2(z),$ for each $z\in\widehat{\mathbb{C}}.$

The following result describes necessary and sufficient conditions to guarantee when two Möbius transformations are identical.

The Möbius transformations $T_{1}$ and $T_{2}$ given by \[ T_1(z)=\dfrac{a_1z+b_1}{c_1z+d_1} \quad \text{and} \quad T_2(z)=\dfrac{a_2z+b_2}{c_2z+d_2}, \] are identical if and only if there exists a nonzero complex number $\lambda\in\mathbb{C}^{\ast}$ such that $a_1=\lambda a_2,$ $b_1=\lambda b_2,$ $c_1=\lambda c_2$ and $d_1=\lambda d_2.$
The necessary condition is immediate. Let us prove the sufficient condition. Take the identical Möbius transformations $T_{1}$ and $T_{2}$, given by the assignment rules \[ T_1(z)=\dfrac{a_1z+b_1}{c_1z+d_1}\quad \text{and} \quad T_2(z)=\dfrac{a_2z+b_2}{c_2z+d_2}, \] whose coefficients are different from zero, (the proof for the case in which some of the coefficients takes the value zero is similar). We evaluate the values $0,$ $1$ and $\infty$ in the transformations and obtain
\begin{align} T_1(0) = T_{2}(0) & =\frac{b_1}{d_1}=\frac{b_2}{d_2}=u,\label{eq:T0}\\ T_{1}(1)=T_{2}(1) &=\frac{a_1+b_1}{c_1+d_1}=\frac{a_2+b_2}{c_2+d_2}\label{eq:T1},\\ T_{1}(\infty)=T_{2}(\infty)& =\frac{a_1}{c_1}=\frac{a_2}{c_2}=v\label{eq:Tinfinito}, \end{align}
for some complex values $u,v\in\mathbb{C}.$ Now, in equations \eqref{eq:T0} and \eqref{eq:Tinfinito} we solve appropriately and obtain the equalities
\[ b_1=ud_1, \quad b_2=ud_2,\quad a_1=vc_1, \quad a_2=vc_2. \]

We substitute these last four relations into equation \eqref{eq:T1}. \[ \frac{vc_1+ud_1}{c_1+d_1} =\frac{vc_2+ud_2}{c_2+d_2}. \]

Then, we compute and thus obtain

\begin{align*} (vc_1+ud_1)(c_2+d_2) & =(vc_2+ud_2)(c_1+d_1),\\ vc_1c_2+vc_1d_2+ud_1c_2+ud_1d_2 & = vc_1c_2+vd_1c_2+uc_1d_2+ud_1d_2,\\ vc_1d_2+ ud_1c_2& =vd_1c_2+uc_1d_2,\\ v(c_1d_2-d_1c_2)-u(c_1d_2-d_1c_2) &=0,\\ (v-u)(c_1d_2-d_1c_2)& =0.\\ \end{align*}
Now, we will argue that the condition $v-u=0$ is not possible and \[ c_1d_2-d_1c_2=0. \] must be satisfied.

Suppose that $v-u=0,$ then from equations (\ref{eq:T0}) and (\ref{eq:T1}) it follows that \[ u-v=\dfrac{a_1}{c_1}-\dfrac{b_1}{d_1}=\dfrac{a_1d_1-b_1c_1}{c_1d_1}=0. \] This last equality implies that the determinant of the Möbius transformation $T_1$ is ${\rm det}(T_{1})=0.$ Therefore, \begin{equation}\label{eq:Mobius_identicas_igualdad_cero} v-u\neq 0 \quad \text{ and } \quad c_1d_2-d_1c_2=0. \end{equation}

Next, we solve in the second equality described in \eqref{eq:Mobius_identicas_igualdad_cero} and obtain \begin{equation}\label{eq:ultima} \frac{c_1}{c_2}=\frac{d_1}{d_2}. \end{equation} Finally, equations \eqref{eq:T0} and \eqref{eq:T1} can be rewritten in the form \[ \dfrac{b_{1}}{b_{2}}=\dfrac{d_{1}}{d_{2}}\quad \text{ and } \quad \dfrac{a_{1}}{a_{2}}=\dfrac{c_{1}}{c_{2}}, \] respectively. From equation \eqref{eq:ultima} it follows that \[ \frac{a_1}{a_2}=\frac{b_1}{b_2}=\frac{c_1}{c_2}=\frac{d_1}{d_2}=\lambda. \]

It would be useful and would optimize the study of Möbius transformations if each of them were identical to one whose determinant is a certain real value, perhaps $1$! Indeed, every Möbius transformation is identical to another Möbius transformation whose determinant is equal to $1.$ The construction of such a transformation is simple. We begin by considering a Möbius transformation $T,$ where its assignment rule is given by \[ T(z)= \dfrac{az+b}{cz+d}. \]

Since the determinant ${\rm det}(T)\neq 0,$ then we define the nonzero complex number \[ \lambda=\frac{1}{\sqrt{\det (T)}}\in\mathbb{C}^{\ast}. \]

Thus, we construct the Möbius transformation $\lambda T,$ given by the assignment rule \[ \lambda T(z)= \dfrac{\lambda az+\lambda b}{\lambda cz+\lambda d}, \] which has determinant ${\rm det}(\lambda T)=1$ and is identical to the Möbius transformation $T.$

One of the implications of the previous fact is that each element of the group of Möbius transformations $\mathcal{M}$ (see equation \eqref{eq:grupo_transformaciones_de_Mobius_notacion}) has determinant equal to $1,$ that is,

\begin{equation}\label{definicion:mobius_determinante_1} \mathcal{M}=\left\{[T] : T \text{ is a Möbius transformation such that } {\rm det}(T)=1\right\}. \end{equation}

Remember! The class $[T]$ consists of the Möbius transformations $T$ and $-T.$ If $T(z)=\dfrac{az+b}{cz+d},$ then $-T(z)=\dfrac{-az-b}{-cz-d}.$

Important! We will abuse language and throughout the text we will refer to the equivalence class $[T]\in \mathcal{M}$ as the Möbius transformation $T.$ If necessary we will specify that we are referring to the transformations $T$ and $-T,$ which are identical.


Properties

Properties

Curiously, any Möbius transformation can be written as the finite composition of the canonical transformations.

Every Möbius transformation can be written as the finite composition of translations, rotations, dilations and inversion.
Take a Möbius transformation $T$ given by the assignment rule \[ T(z)=\dfrac{az+b}{cz+d}. \] We must study the following two cases.

Case 1. If the coefficient $c=0,$ then the assignment rule of the transformation $T$ can be rewritten as follows \[ T(z)=\dfrac{az}{d}+\dfrac{b}{d}. \] Thus, we conclude that $T$ is a composition of a rotation followed by a dilation and a translation.

Case 2. Conversely, if the coefficient $c\neq 0,$ then we can rewrite the correspondence rule of $T$ as follows

\begin{equation}\label{eq:det_no_cero} \begin{split} T(z) &=\frac{az+b}{cz+d}=\frac{az+b+\dfrac{ad}{c}-\dfrac{ad}{c}}{cz+d}=\frac{\dfrac{a}{c}(cz+d)+b-\dfrac{ad}{c}}{cz+d},\\ &=\dfrac{a}{c}+\frac{\dfrac{bc-ad}{c}}{cz+d}. \end{split} \end{equation}
This last equation implies that the Möbius transformation $T$ is a finite composition of translations, rotations, dilations and inversion.

From equation \eqref{eq:det_no_cero} we easily deduce that if the determinant of a Möbius transformation is equal to zero, then it is a constant transformation. More precisely:

If the determinant ${\rm det}(T)$ of a Möbius transformation $T$ is equal to zero, then $T$ is a constant function.

Like the stereographic projection, Möbius transformations also preserve the generalized circles of the extended complex plane $\widehat{\mathbb{C}}.$ Recall that a generalized circle on the extended complex plane $\widehat{\mathbb{C}}$ is a Euclidean circumference in $\mathbb{C}$ or a Euclidean straight line in $\mathbb{C}$ together with the point $\infty$ (see Definition 3, Chap. 1).

Translations, rotations, dilations and inversion preserve generalized circles on the extended complex plane $\widehat{\mathbb{C}}.$
We will verify the theorem for translations and inversion; the remaining cases are left to the reader for verification.

The transformation $T$ is a translation. Consider the generalized circle $\mathcal{C}$ on the extended complex plane $\widehat{\mathbb{C}}$ and the translation given by \[ T(z)=z+z_{0}, \] for some $z_{0}=x_{0}+y_{0}i\in\mathbb{C}^{\ast}.$ We study the following two cases for $\mathcal{C}.$

If $\mathcal{C}$ is a Euclidean line. Suppose that the complex number $z=x+yi$ is in $\mathcal{C},$ then the real numbers $x$ and $y$ satisfy the equation \begin{equation}\label{eq:linea_recta_prueba_teorema} Bx+Cy+D=0, \end{equation} for some real numbers $B,C,D\in\mathbb{R},$ such that $B$ or $C$ is nonzero. Let us see that for the complex number \[ T(z)=z+z_{0}=u+vi \] the real values $u$ and $v$ also satisfy a general equation such as the one described in \eqref{eq:linea_recta_prueba_teorema}. Since

$$T(z)=z+z_{0}= (x+x_{0})+(y+y_{0})i=u+vi,$$
then \[ u=x+x_{0} \quad \text{ and } \quad v=y+y_{0}. \] Now, we substitute these terms into equation \eqref{eq:linea_recta_prueba_teorema}, perform operations and obtain \begin{equation*} \begin{split} B(u-x_{0})+C(v-y_{0})+D&=0,\\ Bu+Cv+(D-Bx_{0}-Cy_{0})&=0. \end{split} \end{equation*} From the previous equality, we can conclude that the image $T(\mathcal{C})$ is also a Euclidean straight line.

If $\mathcal{C}$ is a Euclidean circumference. Suppose that the complex number $z$ lies on $\mathcal{C},$ that is, it satisfies the equation \begin{equation}\label{eq:circunferencia_euclidiana_prueba_teorema} |z-w_{0}|=r, \end{equation} for some complex number $w_{0}\in\mathbb{C}$ and a real number $r>0.$ Let us see that the complex number \[ w=T(z)=z+z_{0}, \] satisfies an equation such as the one described in \eqref{eq:circunferencia_euclidiana_prueba_teorema}. We solve for $z$ in the previous equality, substitute into the equation of the Euclidean circumference $\mathcal{C}$ and obtain \begin{equation*} \begin{split} |z-w_{0}| & =r,\\ |w-z_{0}-w_{0}|&=r,\\ |w-(z_{0}+w_{0})|&=r. \end{split} \end{equation*} From the previous relation, we conclude that the image $T(\mathcal{C})$ consists of a Euclidean circumference with center $(z_{0}+w_{0})$ and radius $r.$

If $T$ is the involution. Take the inversion given by \[ T(z)=\frac{1}{z}, \] and a generalized circle $\mathcal{C}$ on the extended complex plane $\widehat{\mathbb{C}}.$ Let us see that the image $T(\mathcal{C})$ is also a generalized circle. Recall that $\mathcal{C}$ can be represented by the general equation

\begin{equation}\label{eq:circulo_generalizado} A(x^2+y^{2})+2Bx+2Cy+D=0, \end{equation}
where $A,B,C$ and $D$ are real values. If $A=0$ and at least one of the terms $B$ or $C$ is nonzero, then $\mathcal{C}$ is a Euclidean line. Conversely, if $A\neq 0$ and $B^{2}+C^2-AD>0,$ then $\mathcal{C}$ is a Euclidean circumference.

Now, consider the nonzero complex number $z=x+yi$ such that $x$ and $y$ satisfy the equation defined in \eqref{eq:circulo_generalizado}. Let us see that for the complex number \[ T(z)=\dfrac{1}{z}=u+vi \] the real values $u$ and $v$ also satisfy a general equation such as the one described in \eqref{eq:circulo_generalizado}. Since $T(z)=\dfrac{1}{z}=\dfrac{\overline{z}}{|z|^2},$ then

\begin{equation} u=\frac{x}{x^2+y^2}, \quad v=\frac{-y}{x^2+y^2}\quad \text{and} \quad \left|\frac{1}{z}\right|^2=u^2+v^2=\dfrac{1}{x^2+y^2}. \end{equation}
Now, we substitute these terms into equation \eqref{eq:circulo_generalizado} and obtain
\[ A\left(\frac{1}{u^2+v^2}\right)+2B\left(\frac{u}{u^2+v^2}\right)+2C\left(\frac{-v}{u^2+v^2}\right)-D=0. \]
Simplifying the previous expression we obtain the new expression
\begin{equation}\label{eq:circulo_generalizado_aplicar_inmersion} D(u^2+v^2)-2Bu+2Cv-A =0. \end{equation}
We must explore the following cases.

Case 1. If $A=0,$ then the generalized circle $\mathcal{C}$ is a line and its equation is of the form \[ 2Bx+2Cy+D=0, \] such that at least one of the terms $B$ or $C$ is nonzero. From equation \eqref{eq:circulo_generalizado_aplicar_inmersion} it follows that the pairs $(u,v)$ in $T(\mathcal{C})$ satisfy the equality \[ D(u^2+v^2)-2Bu+2Cv=0. \] If $D=0,$ then the image $T(\mathcal{C})$ is a Euclidean straight line. Conversely, if $D\neq 0,$ then in this last expression we complete squares, compute and obtain

\begin{equation*} \begin{split} u^2-\frac{2Bu}{D}+\frac{B^2}{D^2} +v^2+\frac{2Cv}{D}+\frac{C^2}{D^2}&=\frac{B^{2}}{D^{2}}+\frac{C^2}{D^2},\\ \left(u-\frac{B}{D}\right)^2+\left(v+\frac{C}{D}\right)^2&=\frac{B^2+C^2}{D^2}\\ \end{split} \end{equation*}
From the previous equality, we can conclude that the image $T(\mathcal{C})$ is a Euclidean circumference.

Case 2. If $A\neq 0,$ then the generalized circle $\mathcal{C}$ is a Euclidean circumference, whose equation is given by \[ A(x^2+y^2)+2Bx+2Cy+D=0, \] such that $B ^2+C^2-AD>0.$ Then the pairs $(u,v)$ in the image $T(\mathcal{C})$ satisfy the equality \[ D(u^2+v^2)-2Bu+2Cv-A =0. \] If $D=0,$ then the image $T(\mathcal{C})$ is a Euclidean straight line. Conversely, if $D\neq 0,$ we divide both sides of the equality by $D$, complete squares, perform operations and obtain

\begin{equation*} \begin{split} D(u^2+v^2)-2Bu+2Cv-A &=0,\\ u^2-\frac{2Bu}{D}+\frac{B^2}{D^2} +v^2+\frac{2Cv}{D}+\frac{C^2}{D^2}&=\frac{B^{2}}{D^{2}}+\frac{C^2}{D^2}+\frac{A}{D},\\ \left(u-\frac{B}{2D}\right)^2+\left(v+\frac{C}{2D}\right)^2&=\frac{B^2+C^2+AD}{D^2}.\\ \end{split} \end{equation*}
From this last expression, we can conclude that $T(\mathcal{C})$ is a Euclidean circumference, since $B^2+C^2+AD>0.$ The previous arguments prove that inversion sends generalized circles to generalized circles.

As a consequence of the previous result and Theorem 2, the image of any generalized circle under any Möbius transformation is again a generalized circle, i.e., $T(\mathcal{C})$ is a generalized circle of the Extended Complex Plane $\widehat{\mathbb{C}}.$

The group of Möbius transformations $\mathcal{M}$ acts on the set of all generalized circles of the Extended Complex Plane $\widehat{\mathbb{C}}.$

Fixed points

During the study of real analysis, we asked questions about the fixed points of real-valued functions. This is our motivation for studying the fixed points of Möbius transformations. Recall that $z\in \widehat{\mathbb{C}}$ is a fixed point of the Möbius transformation $T,$ if $T(z)=z.$

The first question to address is: how many and which are the fixed points of a Möbius transformation different from the identity? The following exploration will lead us to the conclusion that each Möbius transformation different from the identity has at most two fixed points.

If we take a Möbius transformation $T\neq {\rm Id},$ given by the assignment rule \[ T(z)=\dfrac{az+b}{cz+d}, \] and suppose that $z\in\widehat{\mathbb{C}}$ is a fixed point of $T,$ then we must study the equality $T(z)=z.$

  1. If the coefficient $c=0,$ then $T(\infty)=\infty.$ This means that $z=\infty$ is a fixed point of $T.$ Additionally, when considering $T(z)=z$ with $z\neq\infty,$ we must solve for $z$ in the equality \[ \dfrac{az+b}{d}=z. \] Thus, we conclude that another fixed point of the Möbius transformation is the complex number \[ z=\dfrac{b}{a-d}. \]
  2. Conversely, if the coefficient $c\neq 0,$ then we solve for $z$ in the equation $T(z)=z$ and obtain \begin{equation*} \begin{split} \frac{az+b}{cz+d}&=z,\\ az+b & =cz^2+zd,\\ cz^2+z(d-a)-b&=0.\ \end{split} \end{equation*} The solutions to this last quadratic equation correspond to the fixed points of $T,$ that is, \[ z=\dfrac{-(d-a)\pm\sqrt{(d-a)^2+4cb}}{2c}. \]
The only Möbius transformation that fixes the points $\textbf{0}, 1$ and $\infty$ is the identity.
Suppose that there exists a Möbius transformation $T$ with assignment rule \[ T(z)=\dfrac{az+b}{cz+d}, \] such that $T$ fixes $\textbf{0}, 1$ and $\infty,$ that is \[ T(\textbf{0})=\textbf{0}, \quad T(1)=1 \quad \text{ and } \quad T(\infty)=\infty. \]

Since the transformation $T$ fixes the element $\infty,$ then the coefficient $c=\textbf{0}.$ Therefore the assignment rule is rewritten as \[ T(z)=\dfrac{a}{d}z+\dfrac{b}{d}. \] Now, since $T$ fixes $\textbf{0},$ then \[ T(\textbf{0})=\textbf{0}=\dfrac{b}{d}. \] This last equality implies that $b=0.$ Thus, the assignment rule of $T$ is rewritten as follows \[ T(z)=\dfrac{az}{d}. \]

Finally, since the transformation $T$ fixes $1,$ that is \[ T(1)=\dfrac{a}{d}=1, \] which implies the equality between coefficients $a=d.$ From there, we conclude that the assignment rule of $T$ is given by $T(z)=z,$ for each $z\in\widehat{\mathbb{C}}.$

As a consequence of the previous result, it is always possible for us to construct a Möbius transformation $T$ that sends three different points $z_{1},z_{2},z_{3}\in\widehat{\mathbb{C}}$ to three different points $w_{1},w_{2},w_{3}\in\widehat{\mathbb{C}},$ satisfying $T(z_{i})=w_{i},$ for each $i\in\{1,2,3\}.$

Consider two triples $(z_1,z_2,z_3)$ and $(w_1,w_2,w_3)$ consisting of points of the extended complex plane $\widehat{\mathbb{C}}$ and, moreover, the points that make up each triple are different, then there exists a unique Möbius transformation $T$ such that $T(z_i)=w_i,$ for each $i\in\{1,2,3\}.$
For the construction of the Möbius transformation mentioned in the theorem we will follow a strategy consisting of two steps.

Step 1. We will construct two Möbius transformations $S$ and $M,$ with the following properties:

\begin{equation} \begin{array}{ccc} S(z_1)=\textbf{0},&S(z_2)=1, & S(z_3)=\infty;\\ M(w_1)=\textbf{0}, & M(w_2)=1,& M(w_3)=\infty. \end{array} \end{equation}
Thus, we will obtain the Möbius transformation given by the composition $M^{-1}\circ S,$ which satisfies \[ M^{-1}\circ S(z_{i})=w_{i},\, \text{for each}\, i\in\{1,2,3\}. \]

Consider the case in which none of the points that make up the triple $(z_1,z_2,z_3)$ is $\infty,$ then we define the Möbius transformation $S$ given by the assignment rule \begin{equation}\label{eq:Mobius_S} S(z)=\frac{(z_2-z_3)}{(z_2-z_1)}\frac{(z-z_1)}{(z-z_3)}. \end{equation} Note that the Möbius transformation $S$ sends the points $z_1,$ $z_2$ and $z_3$ to the points $0,$ $1$ and $\infty,$ respectively, i.e., \[ S(z_1)=0,\quad (z_2)=1 \quad \text{ and } \quad S(z_3)=\infty. \] Conversely, if one of the points of the triple $(z_1,z_2,z_3)$ is $z_{i}=\infty,$ for some $i\in\{1,2,3\},$ then we define the transformation $S$ as described in equation \eqref{eq:Mobius_S}, but we suppress the two parentheses in which this term $z_{i}$ appears. In the particular case, if $z_1=\infty,$ then \[ S(z)=\frac{(z_2-z_3)}{(z-z_3)}. \]

Using the previous ideas in the construction of the transformation $S,$ we can define a Möbius transformation $M$ such that

\[ M(w_1)=0,\quad M(w_2)=1 \quad \text{ and } \quad M(w_3)=\infty. \]

Finally, we consider the composition function $M^{-1}\circ S,$ which is also a Möbius transformation that satisfies \[ M^{-1}\circ S(z_i)=w_i, \, \text{for each} \, i\in\{1,2,3\}. \]

Step 2. We will prove that the only function that satisfies the conditions of the theorem is the composition \begin{equation} T=M^{-1}\circ S. \end{equation}

Suppose that there exists another Möbius transformation $\overline{T}$ such that \[ \overline{T}(z_i)=w_i, \, \text{for each} \, i\in\{1,2,3\}. \] Then the composition $\overline{T} \circ M\circ S^{-1}$ is a Möbius transformation that fixes the points $\textbf{0},$ $1$ and $\infty.$ From Theorem (5), it follows that the transformation $T\circ M \circ S^{-1}$ is the identity function $Id,$ that is, $T\circ M \circ S^{-1}(z)=Id(z)$ for each $z\in\widehat{\mathbb{C}}.$ Therefore, $\overline{T}=M^{-1}\circ S.$

From Euclidean geometry we know that three distinct points $z_{1},z_{2},z_{3}$ of the complex plane $\mathbb{C}$ define a Euclidean circumference. Even more, given any three distinct points $z_{1},z_{2},z_{2}$ in the Extended Complex Plane $\widehat{\mathbb{C}}$ we can draw an extended circle that passes through the mentioned three points. Now, if we consider two generalized circles $\mathcal{C}_{1}$ and $\mathcal{C}_{2}$ of the Extended Complex Plane $\widehat{\mathbb{C}},$ then the previous Theorem and Theorem 4 guarantee us the existence of a Möbius transformation $T$ that sends the generalized circle $\mathcal{C}_{1}$ onto the generalized circle $\mathcal{C}_{2}.$ The following theorem states this assertion.

The group of Möbius transformations $\mathcal{M}$ acts transitively on the set of all generalized circles of the Extended Complex Plane $\widehat{\mathbb{C}}.$ In other words, given the generalized circles $\mathcal{C}_1$ and $\mathcal{C}_2$ on $\widehat{\mathbb{C}},$ then there exists a Möbius transformation $T$ that sends the generalized circle $\mathcal{C}_1$ onto the generalized circle $\mathcal{C}_2.$
Consider the triples $$(z_1,z_2,z_3) \quad \text{and} \quad (w_1,w_2,w_3)$$

consisting of different points, such that they lie on the generalized circles $\mathcal{C}_1$ and $\mathcal{C}_2,$ respectively.

From Theorem 6, there exists a Möbius transformation $T$ such that $T(z_i)=w_i$ for each $i\in\{1,2,3\}.$ Since Möbius transformations send generalized circles onto generalized circles (see Theorem 4) and, since $\mathcal{C}_1$ and $\mathcal{C}_2$ are determined by the triples $(z_1,z_2,z_3)$ and $(w_1,w_2,w_3),$ respectively, then $T(\mathcal{C}_1)=\mathcal{C}_2.$

The cross ratio

In the study of Euclidean geometry it is natural to ask about those relations that are invariant under affine transformations. This class of questions can also be extended to the area of Möbius transformations. In the context of projective geometry the concept of cross ratio or double ratio appears (see e.g. Coxeter (1998), p. 77; Cederberg (1995), p. 265), which is invariant under Möbius transformations.

The cross ratio or double ratio associated to four distinct points $z_1,$ $z_2,$ $z_3$ and $z_4$ in $\widehat{\mathbb{C}},$ is \begin{equation}\label{eq:razon_cruzada} (z_1,z_2;z_3,z_4):=\frac{(z_1-z_3)(z_2-z_4)}{(z_1-z_2)(z_3-z_4)}. \end{equation} If some $z_j$ is $\infty,$ then the cross ratio is given by \begin{equation*} \begin{split} (\infty,z_2;z_3,z_4)=& \frac{(z_3-z_4)}{(z_2-z_3)},\\ (z_1,\infty;z_3,z_4)=&-\frac{(z_3-z_4)}{(z_4-z_1)},\\ (z_1,z_2;\infty,z_4)=&-\frac{(z_1-z_2)}{(z_4-z_1)},\\ (z_1,z_2;z_3,\infty)=&\frac{(z_1-z_2)}{(z_2-z_3)}.\\ \end{split} \end{equation*}

By fixing the three points $z_2,$ $z_3$ and $z_4$ of the cross ratio we can define the Möbius transformation \[ T(z)=(z,z_2;z_3,z_4)=\frac{(z-z_3)(z_2-z_4)}{(z-z_2)(z_3-z_4)}, \] which sends $z_2,$ $z_3$ and $z_4$ to $\infty,$ $0$ and $1,$ respectively.

Möbius transformations preserve the cross ratio, that is, if, given the Möbius transformation $T$ and the four distinct points $z_{1},z_{2},z_{3},z_{4}\in\widehat{\mathbb{C}},$ then the equality \[ (z_1,z_2,z_3,z_4)=(T(z_1),T(z_2);T(z_3),T(z_4)). \] is satisfied.
The following computation is necessary for the verification of the equality. Take the Möbius transformation $T$ given by \[ T(z)=\dfrac{az+b}{cz+d}, \] then for any $i\neq j\in\{1,2,3,4\}$ we have
\begin{equation}\label{t:cuentas_razon_cruzada} \begin{split} T(z_{i})-T(z_{j}) &=\dfrac{az_{i}+b}{cz_{i}+d}-\frac{az_{j}+b}{cz_{j}+d},\\ &\\ &= \frac{(az_{i}+b)(cz_{j}+d)-(az_{j}+b)(cz_{i}+d)}{(cz_{i}+d)(cz_{j}+d)},\\ &\\ &=\frac{\cancel{\color{red}acz_{i}z_{j}}+adz_{i}+bcz_{j}+\cancel{\color{blue}bd}\cancel{-\color{red}acz_{i}z_{j}}-adz_{j}-bcz_{i}\cancel{\color{blue}-bd}}{(cz_{i}+d)(cz_{j}+d)},\\ &\\ &=\frac{z_{i}(ad-bc)-z_{j}(ad-bc)}{(cz_{i}+d)(cz_{j}+d)},\\ &\\ &=\frac{z_{i}-z_{j}}{(cz_{i}+d)(cz_{j}+d)}.\\ \end{split} \end{equation}

Substituting the previous relation into the definition of cross ratio, we have

\begin{equation} \begin{split} (T(z_1),T(z_2);T(z_3),T(z_4))&= \dfrac{(T(z_1)-T(z_3))(T(z_2)-T(z_4))}{(T(z_1)-T(z_2))(T(z_3)-T(z_4))},\\ &\\ &=\dfrac{\dfrac{(z_{1}-z_{3})}{\cancel{\color{red}(cz_{1}+d)}\cancel{\color{blue}(cz_{3}+d)}}\dfrac{(z_{2}-z_{4})}{\cancel{\color{orange}(cz_{2}+d)}\cancel{\color{pink}(cz_{4}+d)}}}{\dfrac{(z_{1}-z_{2})}{\cancel{\color{red}(cz_{1}+d)}\cancel{\color{orange}(cz_{2}+d)}}\dfrac{(z_{3}-z_{4})}{\cancel{\color{blue}(cz_{3}+d)}\cancel{\color{pink}(cz_{4}+d)}}},\\ &\\ &=\dfrac{(z_{1}-z_{3})(z_{2}-z_{4})}{(z_{1}-z_{2})(z_{3}-z_{4})},\\ &\\ &=(z_{1},z_{2};z_{3},z_{4}). \end{split} \end{equation}


Möbius transformations as matrices

Möbius transformations as matrices

In some cases it is difficult to deal with groups of functions and, to circumvent the inconveniences that algebra indicates to us, perhaps we can understand this group from another perspective or a better representation. We will identify the group of Möbius transformations $\mathcal{M}$ (see equation \eqref{definicion:mobius_determinante_1}) with a quotient group of matrices known as the Projective Special Linear Group ${\rm PSL}(2,\mathbb{C}).$ Recall that the set $\mathfrak{M}$ consisting of the $2\times 2$ matrices \[ A=\left (\begin{matrix} a & b \\ c & d \end{matrix}\right), \] with coefficients $a,b,c,d\in\mathbb{C},$ has the structure of an $\mathbb{R}$-vector space. Naturally, we identify $\mathfrak{M}$ with $\mathbb{C}^4.$ More precisely, to the matrix $A=\left (\begin{matrix} a & b \\ c & d \end{matrix}\right) $ we associate the $4$-tuple $(a,b,c,d).$ Thus, $\mathfrak{M}$ is endowed with the topology induced by the norm \[ |A|:=\sqrt{|a|^2+|b|^2+|c|^2+|d|^2}. \]

On the other hand, the determinant function ${\rm det}: \mathfrak{M}\to \mathbb{C},$ defined by \begin{equation}\label{eq:funcion_determinante} {\rm det}\left (\begin{matrix} a & b \\ c & d \end{matrix}\right)=ad-bc, \end{equation} is a continuous map; since it is a complex polynomial function of four variables. The inverse image of the open subset $\mathbb{C}^{\ast}$ under ${\rm det}$ \[ {\rm det}^{-1}(\mathbb{C}^{\ast})=\{A\in \mathfrak{M}:{\rm det}(A)\neq 0\}, \] is an open subset of $\mathfrak{M},$ known as the General Linear Group.

The General Linear Group ${\rm GL}(2,\mathbb{C})$ is the set consisting of the $2\times 2$ matrices \[ A=\left (\begin{matrix} a & b \\ c & d \end{matrix}\right), \] with coefficients $a,b,c,d\in\mathbb{C}$ and determinant ${\rm det}(A)=ad-bc\neq \textbf{0},$ together with the usual matrix multiplication operation.

Recall that the open subset $\mathbb{C}^{\ast}$ of $\mathbb{C}$ with complex multiplication is a group. Using the properties of the determinant of matrices one verifies that the determinant function \begin{equation}\label{eq:funcion_determinante_homomorfismo} {\rm det}:{\rm GL}(2,\mathbb{C})\to \mathbb{C}^{\ast}, \end{equation} is a surjective group homomorphism. From the First Group Isomorphism Theorem, it follows that the kernel of the determinant is a subgroup

\[ {\rm ker}({\rm det})=\left\{A\in {\rm GL}(2,\mathbb{C}): {\rm det}(A)=1\right\}\lt{\rm GL}(2,\mathbb{C}), \]
and is known as the Special Linear Group.

The Special Linear Group ${\rm SL}(2,\mathbb{C})$ is the set consisting of the $2\times 2$ matrices \[ A=\left (\begin{matrix} a & b \\ c & d \end{matrix}\right), \] with coefficients $a,b,c,d\in\mathbb{C}$ and determinant ${\rm det}(A)=ad-bc=1,$ together with the usual matrix multiplication operation.

Now, we consider the multiplicative group $\{1,-1\},$ which acts on ${\rm SL}(2,\mathbb{C})$ by scalar multiplication. More precisely, the function $\alpha:\{1,-1\}\times {\rm SL}(2,\mathbb{C})\to {\rm SL}(2\mathbb{C}),$ given by \[ \alpha(\pm 1,A)=\pm 1A \] is an action. The quotient space \[ {\rm PSL}(2,\mathbb{C}):={\rm SL}(2,\mathbb{C})/\{1,-1\}, \] has a group structure with the usual matrix multiplication operation between representatives of the classes, i.e., \begin{equation*} [A][B]:=[AB], \end{equation*} for any two classes $[A],[B]\in {\rm PSL}(2,\mathbb{C}).$ This new group is known as the Projective Special Linear Group.

The Projective Special Linear Group is the quotient space \[ {\rm PSL}(2,\mathbb{C}):={\rm SL}(2,\mathbb{C})/\{1,-1\} \] with the binary operation given by \begin{equation}\label{eq:proyectivo_lineal_especial} [A][B]:=[AB], \end{equation} for any two classes $[A],[B]\in {\rm PSL}(2,\mathbb{C}).$

Note that the class $[A]\in{\rm PSL}(2,\mathbb{C})$ consists of the two matrices \[ [A]=\{A,-A\}. \]

We are now prepared to identify the group of Möbius transformations $\mathcal{M}$ with the Projective Special Linear Group ${\rm PSL}(2,\mathbb{C}).$ Naturally, given the class $[A]\in{\rm PSL}(2,\mathbb{C}),$ the coefficients of a matrix $A$ can be thought of as the coefficients of a Möbius transformation $T_{A}.$ More precisely, the matrix \[ A=\left (\begin{matrix} a & b \\ c & d \end{matrix}\right), \] defines the Möbius transformation $T_{A}$ given by the assignment rule \[ T_{A}(z)=\dfrac{az+b}{cz+d}. \] Note that the matrix \[ -A=\left (\begin{matrix} -a & -b \\ -c & -d \end{matrix}\right), \] defines the Möbius transformation $T_{-A}$ given by \[ T_{-A}(z)=\dfrac{-az-b}{-cz-d}=\dfrac{-(az+b)}{-(cz+d)}=T_{A}(z). \]

However, both matrices $A,-A$ belonging to the class $[A]$ define Möbius transformations $T_{A}$ and $T_{-A},$ respectively, which are identical and, we denote this class by $T_{[A]}.$ Therefore, the class $[A]\in{\rm PSL}(2,\mathbb{C})$ defines the Möbius transformation $T_{[A]}.$ Thus, we have the function $\psi: {\rm PSL}(2,\mathbb{C})\to \mathcal{M},$ given by \[ \psi([A])=T_{[A]}. \]

The function $\psi : {\rm PSL}(2,\mathbb{C}) \to \mathcal{M}$ is a group isomorphism.
First we will verify that $\psi$ is a group homomorphism. Consider the matrix classes \[ [A_{1}]=\left[\begin{matrix} a_1 & b_1 \\ c_1 & d_1 \end{matrix}\right] \quad \text{and } \quad [A_{2}]=\left[\begin{matrix} a_2 & b_2 \\ c_2 & d_2 \end{matrix}\right] \] in ${\rm PSL}(2,\mathbb{C})$ and let us see that the equality \[ \psi([A_{1}][A_{2}])=\psi([A_{1}A_{2}])=\psi([A_1])\circ \psi ([A_2]). \] holds.

Since the product of matrix classes $[A_{1}][A_{2}]=[A_{1} A_{2}]$ is given by \[ [A_{1} A_{2}]=\left[\begin{matrix} a_1a_2+b_1c_2 & a_1b_2+b_1d_2 \\ c_1a_2+d_1c_2& c_1b_2+d_1d_2 \end{matrix}\right], \] then $\psi$ assigns to the class $[A_{1} A_{2}]$ the Möbius transformation $\psi([A_{1} A_{2}])=T_{[A_{1} A_{2}]},$ which is defined by

\begin{equation}\label{eq:psi_1} T_{[A_{1} A_{2}]}(z)=\dfrac{(a_1a_2+b_1c_2)z+(a_1b_2+b_1d_2)}{(c_1a_2+d_1c_2)z+(c_1b_2+d_1d_2)}. \end{equation}
On the other hand, $\psi$ assigns to the matrix classes $[A_{1}]$ and $[A_{2}]$ the Möbius transformations $T_{[A_1]}$ and $T_{[A_2]},$ respectively, where
\begin{equation*} T_{[A_{1}]}(z)=\dfrac{a_1z+b_1}{c_1z+d_1} \quad \text{ and } \quad T_{[A_{2}]}(z)=\dfrac{a_2z+b_2}{c_2z+d_2}. \end{equation*}
Then, the composition function $\psi([A_{1}])\circ \psi([A_{2}])=T_{[A_{1}]}\circ T_{[A_{2}]},$ where
\begin{equation}\label{eq:psi_2} \begin{split} T_{[A_{1}]}\circ T_{[A_{2}]}(z)&= \dfrac{a_1\left(\dfrac{a_2z+b_2}{c_2z+d_2}\right)+b_1}{c_1\left(\dfrac{a_2z+b_2}{c_2z+d_2}\right)+d_1},\\ &\\ &=\dfrac{\dfrac{a_{1}(a_2z+b_2)+b_{1}(c_2z+d_2)}{\cancel{\color{red}c_2z+d_2}}}{\dfrac{c_{1}(a_2z+b_2)+d_{1}(c_2z+d_2)}{\cancel{\color{red}c_2z+d_2}}},\\ &\\ &= \frac{(a_1a_2+b_1c_2)z+(a_1b_2+b_1d_2) }{(c_1a_2+d_1c_2)z+(c_1b_2+d_1d_2)}. \end{split} \end{equation}
Comparing equations \eqref{eq:psi_1} and \eqref{eq:psi_2} we conclude that
\[ \psi([A_{1} A_{2}])=T_{[A_{1} A_{2}]}=T_{[A_1]}\circ T_{[A_2]}=\psi([A_1])\circ \psi([A_2]). \]
In other words, the function $\psi$ is a group homomorphism.

Surjectivity. It is very easy to verify. If we consider a Möbius transformation $T\in \mathcal{M},$ where $T$ is given by the assignment rule \[ T(z)=\dfrac{az+b}{cz+d}, \] it is enough to take the matrix class given by the coefficients of the transformation, that is \[ [A]=\left[\begin{matrix} a & b \\ c & d \end{matrix}\right]. \] For this class we have $\psi([A])=[T].$

Injectivity. We will prove that the kernel of $\psi$ consists only of the class of the identity matrix i.e., $\rm Ker(\psi)=\{[\rm Id]=\{\rm Id,-\rm Id\}\}.$ Take the matrix class \[ [A]=\left[\begin{matrix} a & b \\ c & d \end{matrix}\right]\in {\rm Ker}(\psi), \] then $\psi([A])=T_{[A]}$ is the class consisting of the identity transformation and minus identity, that is, \[ T_{A}(z)={\rm Id}(z)=\dfrac{az +b}{ cz+d}=\dfrac{\pm 1z+0}{0z+\pm 1}. \] From this last relation it follows that the coefficients of the Möbius transformation $T$ satisfy the equalities \[ b=c=0 \quad \text{ and } \quad a=d=\pm 1. \] Therefore, the kernel ${\rm Ker}(\psi)$ consists of the class of the identity matrix $[\rm Id],$ that is, ${\rm Ker}(\psi)=\{[\rm Id]\}.$


Classification of Möbius transformations

Classification of Möbius transformations

Henceforth we will abuse language and refer to the equivalence class $[T]\in \mathcal{M}$ as the Möbius transformation $T.$

We can classify Möbius transformations by conjugation and trace. From either of these points of view, each transformation is placed as parabolic, elliptic, hyperbolic or loxodromic. This assignment is motivated by the classification of conics.

For the conic $Q$ with eccentricity $\varepsilon$ we have:
  1. If $\varepsilon\lt 1,$ then the conic $Q$ is an ellipse.
  2. $\varepsilon=1,$ then the conic $Q$ is a parabola.
  3. $\varepsilon \gt 1,$ then the conic $Q$ is a hyperbola.

Recall that all the points of a conic $Q\subset\mathbb{R}^{2}$ satisfy that the quotient between their distances to a fixed point (focus) and to a fixed line (directrix) is always the same number $\varepsilon.$ This number is known as the eccentricity of the conic.

Classification by conjugation

Classification by conjugation

Recall that any Möbius transformation $T$ different from the identity has at most two fixed points. We will conjugate $T$ to understand it as a translation or a dilation. More precisely, we will appropriately construct another Möbius transformation $M$ such that $M^{-1} \circ T\circ M$ is a translation or a dilation.

Consider the Möbius transformations $T$ and $M.$ We say that $T$ is conjugate to $M$ in ${\rm PSL}(2,\mathbb{C}),$ if there exists a transformation $\varphi \in{\rm PSL}(2,\mathbb{C})$ such that $\varphi^{-1}\circ T \circ \varphi=M.$

To begin, let us introduce a result that relates the fixed points of a Möbius transformation and its conjugate.

Consider the Möbius transformations $T$ and $\varphi.$ The point $z_0\in\widehat{\mathbb{C}}$ is a fixed point of the Möbius transformation $\varphi^{-1}\circ T\circ \varphi,$ if and only if, $\varphi(z_0)$ is a fixed point of $T.$
The element $z_0\in\widehat{\mathbb{C}}$ is a fixed point of the composition $\varphi^{-1}\circ T\circ \varphi,$ if and only if, $\varphi^{-1}\circ T\circ \varphi(z_0)=z_0,$ if and only if, $T\circ \varphi(z_0)=\varphi(z_0),$ if and only if, $\varphi(z_0)$ is a fixed point of $T.$
If $T$ is a Möbius transformation different from the identity, then:
  1. If $T$ fixes exactly one point, then $T$ is conjugate in ${\rm PSL}(2,\mathbb{C})$ to a translation. In other words, there exists an element $\varphi\in{\rm PSL}(2,\mathbb{C})$ such that $\varphi^{-1}\circ T\circ \varphi$ is given by \[ \varphi^{-1}\circ T\circ \varphi(z)=z+b, \] for some $b\in\mathbb{C}^{\ast}.$
  2. Conversely, if $T$ fixes exactly two points, then there exists an element $\varphi\in{\rm PSL}(2,\mathbb{C})$ such that the conjugation $\varphi^{-1}\circ T\circ \varphi$ is given by the formula \[ \varphi^{-1}\circ T\circ \varphi(z)= \alpha z, \] for some $\alpha\in\mathbb{C}^{\ast}.$
Consider the Möbius transformation $T$ and suppose that $T$ has exactly one fixed point $z_0\neq \infty.$ Then consider the Möbius transformation $\varphi$ given by \[ \varphi(z)=\frac{1}{z-z_0}, \] which sends $z_0$ to $\infty.$ Since $\varphi^{-1}(\infty)=z_{0}$ is a fixed point of $T,$ from Lemma 4 it follows that $\infty$ is a fixed point of the composition \[ \varphi\circ T \circ \varphi^{-1}. \]

This fact implies that the previous composition is of the form \[ \varphi\circ T\circ \varphi^{-1}(z)=a z+b, \] for some $a,b\in\mathbb{C}^{\ast}.$ Since the transformation $T$ has only one fixed point, then $\varphi\circ T \circ \varphi^{-1}$ also has a unique fixed point, which implies that its assignment rule is given by \[ \varphi^{-1}\circ T\circ\varphi (z)=z+b. \]

Conversely, suppose that the Möbius transformation $T$ has exactly two fixed points $z_0\neq z_1\in \widehat{\mathbb{C}}.$ Then consider the Möbius transformation $\varphi$ given by \[ \varphi(z)= \frac{z-z_0}{z-z_1}, \] which sends $z_0$ to $\textbf{0}$ and $z_1$ to $\infty,$ respectively. Since $\varphi^{-1}(\textbf{0})=z_{0}$ and $\varphi^{-1}(\infty)=z_{1}$ are fixed points of $T,$ from Lemma 4 it follows that the Möbius transformation $\varphi^{-1} \circ T \circ \varphi$ has $\textbf{0}$ and $\infty$ as fixed points. This implies that the assignment rule of this composition is given by \[ \varphi^{-1} \circ T \circ \varphi(z)=\alpha z, \] for some $\alpha\in\mathbb{C}^{\ast}.$

The Möbius transformation $T\neq {\rm Id}$ is called parabolic if it is conjugate in ${\rm PSL}(2,\mathbb{C})$ to a translation. Conversely, if the Möbius transformation $T\neq {\rm Id}$ is conjugate in $PSL(2,\mathbb{C})$ to a transformation $M$ given by \[ M(z)= \alpha z, \] for some $\alpha\in\mathbb{C}^{\ast}.$ Then:
  1. If $|\alpha|=1$ and $\alpha \neq 1,$ we say that $T$ is elliptic.
  2. If $\alpha \in \mathbb{R},$ we call $T$ hyperbolic.
  3. If $|\alpha|\neq 1$ and $\alpha\notin \mathbb{R},$ we say that $T$ is loxodromic.

Classification by trace

Classification by trace

Important! Throughout this section, unless we specify otherwise, we will identify the group of Möbius transformations $\mathcal{M}$ with the group ${\rm PSL}(2,\mathbb{C}).$

The trace of the matrix \[ A=\left (\begin{matrix} a & b \\ c & d \end{matrix}\right), \] in $\mathfrak{M}$ is the sum of the terms on its main diagonal, that is, \[ {\rm tr}(A)=a+d. \] Some of the basic properties of the trace are stated below. We invite the reader to verify them.

Given the matrices $A$ and $B$ in ${\rm GL}(2,\mathbb{C}),$ then the following properties of the trace are satisfied:
  1. ${\rm tr}(A+B)={\rm tr}(A)+{\rm tr}(B).$
  2. ${\rm tr}(\alpha A)=\alpha{\rm tr}(A),$ where $\alpha$ is any nonzero complex number.
  3. ${\rm tr}(AB)={\rm tr}(A){\rm tr}(B).$

Using property 3 stated above and computing, we can prove that the trace is invariant under conjugation in ${\rm GL}(2,\mathbb{C}).$ More precisely:

Given the matrices $A_{1}$ and $A_{2}$ in ${\rm GL}(2,\mathbb{C}),$ then \[ {\rm tr}(A_{2}^{-1}A_{1}A_{2})={\rm tr }(A_{1}). \]
Note that the equality ${\rm tr}(A_{2}^{-1}A_{1}A_{2})={\rm tr}(A_{1})$ is true if and only if the equality
\begin{equation} \begin{split} {\rm tr}(A_{2}){\rm tr}(A_{2}^{-1}A_{1}A_{2})&={\rm tr}(A_{2}){\rm tr}(A_{1}),\\ {\rm tr}(A_{2}A_{2}^{-1}A_{1}A_{2})&={\rm tr}(A_{2}A_{1}),\\ {\rm tr}(A_{1}A_{2})&={\rm tr}(A_{2}A_{1}). \end{split} \end{equation}

To prove the result, it is enough to verify that the traces of the matrix products $A_{1}A_{2}$ and $A_{2}A_{1}$ coincide. If $A_{1}=\left (\begin{matrix} a_{1} & b_{1} \\ c_{1} & d_{1} \end{matrix}\right)$ and $A_{2}=\left (\begin{matrix} a_{2} & b_{2} \\ c_{2} & d_{2} \end{matrix}\right) ,$ then

\[ A_{1} A_{2}=\left[\begin{matrix} {\color{red}a_{1}a_{2}}+{\color{blue}b_{1}c_{2}} & a_{1}b_{2}+b_{1}d_{2} \\ c_{1}a_{2}+d_{1}c_{2}& {\color{orange}c_{1}b_{2}}+{\color{green}d_{1}d_{2}} \end{matrix}\right] \quad \text{ and } \quad A_{2} A_{1}=\left[\begin{matrix} {\color{red} a_{2}a_{1}}+{\color{orange}b_{2}c_{1}} & a_{2}b_{1}+b_{2}d_{1} \\ c_{2}a_{1}+d_{2}c_{1}& {\color{blue}c_{2}b_{1}}+{\color{green}d_{2}d_{1}} \end{matrix}\right]. \]

From the previous computation, it follows that the trace of the matrix products $A_{1}A_{2}$ and $A_{2}A_{1}$ satisfy

\[ {\rm tr}(A_{1}A_{2})={\color{red}a_{1}a_{2}}+{\color{blue}b_{1}c_{2}}+{\color{orange}c_{1}b_{2}}+{\color{green}d_{1}d_{2}}={\rm tr}(A_{2}A_{1}). \]


It is not possible to naturally extend the concept of trace to a class of matrices $[A]$ of ${\rm PSL}(2,\mathbb{R}),$ because the values of the traces of the matrices in such classes do not coincide. More precisely, the class $[A]\in{\rm PSL}(2,\mathbb{C})$ consists of the two matrices \[ [A]=\{A,-A\}, \] where

\[ {\rm tr}(A)=a+d \quad \text{ and } \quad {\rm tr }(-A)=-(a+d)=-{\rm tr}(A). \]
This means that the trace of the class $[A]$ takes two values \[ {\rm tr}[A]=\pm (a+d), \] and therefore, it is not well defined. However, to avoid this inconvenience, we can define the value \[ {\color{blue}\rm Tr}[A]=({\rm tr}(A))^{2}=(a+d)^{2}. \]

The trace of the class $[A]\in {\rm PSL}(2,\mathbb{C})$ is the value \[ {\color{blue} \rm Tr}[A]=({\rm tr}(A))^{2}. \]

Let us understand the trace of a Möbius transformation from its multipliers. Recall that a parabolic Möbius transformation $T$ is conjugate to a translation $M,$ given by \[ M(z)=z+b, \] for some nonzero complex number $b.$ This transformation $M$ is determined by the class of the matrix \begin{equation}\label{ec:multiplicadores_parabolicas} \left (\begin{matrix} 1 & b \\ 0 & 1 \end{matrix}\right) \end{equation} in ${\rm PSL}(2,\mathbb{C}).$ Conversely, if the Möbius transformation $T$ has two fixed points, then it is conjugate to a Möbius transformation $M,$ given by \[ M(z)=\alpha z, \] for some nonzero complex number $\alpha\neq 1.$ This transformation $M$ is determined by the class of the matrix \begin{equation}\label{ec:multiplicadores_no_parabolicas} \left (\begin{matrix} \sqrt{\alpha} & 0 \\ 0 & \dfrac{1}{\sqrt{\alpha}} \end{matrix}\right). \end{equation} in ${\rm PSL}(2,\mathbb{C}).$ The squared terms that appear on the main diagonal of the matrices that represent the different Möbius transformations (see equations \eqref{ec:multiplicadores_parabolicas} and \eqref{ec:multiplicadores_no_parabolicas}) are known as multipliers.

Given the Möbius transformation $T$ different from the identity. Then
  1. If $T$ is parabolic, then its multiplier is defined as $\alpha=1.$
  2. If $T$ is conjugate to the transformation $M(z)=\alpha z,$ for some nonzero complex number $\alpha\neq 1,$ then the values $\alpha$ and $\dfrac{1}{\alpha}$ are the multipliers of $T.$

Since the trace of a matrix is invariant under conjugation, then

\begin{equation}\label{ec:relacion_multiplicadores_traza} \begin{split} {\color{blue}\rm Tr}(T)={\color{blue}\rm Tr}(M)=({\rm tr}(M))^{2}&=\left(\sqrt{\alpha}+\frac{1}{\sqrt{\alpha}}\right)^{2},\\ &=\alpha+2+\frac{1}{\alpha}.\\ \end{split} \end{equation}

Consider the Möbius transformation $T$ with trace ${\color{blue}\rm Tr}[T].$ Then
  1. $T$ is parabolic, if and only if, ${\color{blue}\rm Tr}[T]=({\rm tr}(T))^{2}=4.$
  2. $T$ is hyperbolic, if and only if, ${\color{blue}\rm Tr}[T]=({\rm tr}(T))^{2}>4.$
  3. $T$ is elliptic, if and only if, ${\color{blue}\rm Tr}[T]=({\rm tr}(T))^{2}\lt4.$
  4. $T$ is loxodromic, if and only if, ${\color{blue}\rm Tr}[T]=({\rm tr}(T))^{2} \notin [0,\infty].$
We will begin by proving the sufficient condition of each item.
  1. Suppose that $T$ is parabolic, since the multiplier of $T$ is $\alpha=1,$ then from equation \eqref{ec:relacion_multiplicadores_traza} it follows that \[ {\color{blue}\rm Tr}(T)=1+2+\frac{1}{1}=4. \]
  2. Suppose that $T$ is hyperbolic, then it is conjugate to a transformation $M$ given by \[ M(z)=\alpha z=\dfrac{\sqrt{r} z+0}{0z+1/\sqrt{r}}, \] where $r$ is a positive real number. Note that the transformation $M$ is determined by the class of the matrix \[ \left (\begin{matrix} \sqrt{r} & 0 \\ 0 & 1/\sqrt{r} \end{matrix}\right). \] in ${\rm PSL}(2,\mathbb{C}).$ Thus, the multipliers of $T$ are $\sqrt{r}$ and $\dfrac{1}{\sqrt{r}}.$ From equation \eqref{ec:relacion_multiplicadores_traza} it follows that \begin{equation*} {\color{blue}\rm Tr}(T)=r+2+\frac{1}{r}. \end{equation*} Now, we will verify the inequality \[ r+2+\frac{1}{r}>4, \] using the relation $\left(\sqrt{r}-\dfrac{1}{\sqrt{r}}\right)^{2}>0.$ Then \begin{align} \left(\sqrt{r}-\frac{1}{\sqrt{r}}\right)^{2}&>0,\notag\\ r-2+\frac{1}{r}&>0,\label{ec:diferencia_multiplicadores}\\ r-2+\frac{1}{r}+{\color{red}4}&>{\color{red}4},\notag\\ r+2+\frac{1}{r}&>4.\notag \end{align}
  3. Suppose that $T$ is elliptic, then it is conjugate to a Möbius transformation $M,$ given by \[ M(z)={\rm e}^{i\theta} z= \dfrac{ \rm e^{i\theta/2}z+0 }{ 0z+\rm e^{-i\theta/2} } , \] for some $\theta\in (0,2\pi).$ Note that the transformation $M$ is determined by the class of the matrix \[ \left (\begin{matrix} {\rm e}^{i\theta/2} & 0 \\ 0 & {\rm e}^{-i\theta/2} \end{matrix}\right). \] in ${\rm PSL}(2,\mathbb{C}).$ Since the trace of a matrix is invariant under conjugation, then
    \begin{equation*} \begin{split} {\color{blue}\rm Tr}(T)={\color{blue}\rm Tr}(M)=({\rm tr}(M))^{2}&=({\rm e}^{i\theta/2}+{\rm e}^{-i\theta/2})^{2},\\ &=(\cos (\theta/2)+i\,\sin (\theta/2)+\cos (-\theta/2)+i\,\sin (-\theta/2))^{2},\\ &=(\cos (\theta/2)+\cancel{\color{red}i\,\sin (\theta/2)}+\cos (\theta/2)-\cancel{\color{red}i\,\sin (\theta/2)})^{2},\\ &=(2\cos(\theta/2))^{2}. \end{split} \end{equation*}
    Since $2\cos(\theta/2)\in (-2,2),$ for $\theta \in (0,2\pi),$ then ${\color{blue}\rm Tr}(T)\lt 4.$
  4. Suppose that $T$ is loxodromic, then it is conjugate to a translation $M$ given by \[ M(z)=r{\rm e}^{i\theta}z=\dfrac{\sqrt{r} \rm e ^{i\theta/2}z+0}{0z+\dfrac{\rm e^{-i\theta/2}}{\sqrt{r}}}, \] for some positive real $r\neq 1$ and some $\theta\in(0,2\pi).$ Note that the transformation $M$ is determined by the class of the matrix \[ \left (\begin{matrix} \sqrt{r}\rm e^{i\theta/2} & 0 \\ 0 & \dfrac{\rm e^{-i\theta/2}}{\sqrt{r}} \end{matrix}\right). \] in ${\rm PSL}(2,\mathbb{C}).$ Thus, the multipliers of $T$ are $r{\rm e}^{i\theta}$ and $\dfrac{1}{r}e^{-i\theta}.$ From equation \eqref{ec:relacion_multiplicadores_traza} it follows that
    \begin{equation*} \begin{split} {\color{blue}\rm Tr}(T)&=r{\rm e}^{i\theta}+2+\frac{1}{r}e^{-i\theta},\\ &=r\left(\cos\theta+i\,\sin\theta\right)+2+\frac{1}{r}\left(\cos\theta-i\,\sin\theta\right),\\ &=\left(r+\frac{1}{r}\right)\cos\theta+2+i\left(r-\frac{1}{r}\right)\sin\theta. \end{split} \end{equation*}
    We will argue that $\left(r-\dfrac{1}{r}\right)\sin\theta\neq 0.$ Suppose that $\left(r-\dfrac{1}{r}\right)\sin\theta= 0$ and $\left(r+\dfrac{1}{r}\right)\cos\theta+2$ is a nonnegative real number. Then \[ r-\dfrac{1}{r}=0 \quad \text{ or } \quad \sin\theta= 0. \] For the first case, we would have $r=1,$ but for loxodromic transformations we have $r\neq 1.$ Now, for the next case we have $\sin \theta=0$ and $\theta=\pi.$ This last equality implies that
    \begin{equation}\label{ec:desigualdad_caso_loxodromico_traza} \left(1+\frac{1}{r}\right)\cos\pi+2=-\left(r+\frac{1}{r}\right)+2, \end{equation}
    which is nonnegative. However, from equation \eqref{ec:diferencia_multiplicadores} we have \[ r+\frac{1}{r}>2, \] this last inequality implies that the term described in equation \eqref{ec:desigualdad_caso_loxodromico_traza} is negative.

Finally, we will prove the necessary condition of the items.

  1. Suppose that ${\color{blue}\rm Tr}(T)=4.$ Using the relation described in \eqref{ec:relacion_multiplicadores_traza} it follows that the multipliers of $T$ satisfy \begin{equation} \begin{split} {\color{blue}\rm Tr}(T)&=\alpha+2+\frac{1}{\alpha}=4. \end{split} \end{equation} This implies that $\alpha$ satisfies the equation \begin{equation} \begin{split} \alpha^{2}+2\alpha+1&=4\alpha,\\ \alpha^{2}-2\alpha +1&=0,\\ (\alpha-1)^{2}&=0.\\ \end{split} \end{equation} Therefore, the multipliers of $T$ are $1$ and $\dfrac{1}{1},$ respectively. Since elliptic, hyperbolic and loxodromic transformations do not have these values as multipliers, then $T$ must be parabolic.
  2. Assume that ${\color{blue}\rm Tr}(T)>4.$ Using the relation described in \eqref{ec:relacion_multiplicadores_traza} it follows that the multipliers of $T$ satisfy \begin{equation} \begin{split} {\color{blue}\rm Tr}(T)&=\alpha+2+\frac{1}{\alpha}>4. \end{split} \end{equation} This implies that $\alpha$ is a complex number that satisfies the inequality \begin{equation} \begin{split} \alpha^{2}+2\alpha+1&>4\alpha,\\ \alpha^{2}-2\alpha +1&>0,\\ (\alpha-1)^{2}&>0.\\ \end{split} \end{equation} Therefore, $\alpha$ is a real number such that $\alpha \in (-\infty,1)\cup (1,\infty).$ Since elliptic and loxodromic transformations do not have real numbers as multipliers, then $T$ must be a hyperbolic transformation.
  3. Suppose that ${\color{blue}\rm Tr}(T)\lt 4,$ then the multipliers of the Möbius transformation $T$ must satisfy the inequality \[ \alpha +2 +\dfrac{1}{\alpha}\lt4. \] From items $\textbf{1.}$ and $\textbf{2.}$ already proved, it follows that $\alpha=r{\rm e}^{i\theta},$ for some $r>0$ and some $\theta\in (0,2\pi).$ If $r\neq1,$ that is, $T$ is loxodromic, then from the sufficient condition of item $\textbf{4.},$ it follows that $\alpha +2 +\dfrac{1}{\alpha}$ is a complex number that does not belong to the interval $[0,\infty].$ This fact is a contradiction, therefore, $T$ is elliptic.
  4. Assume that ${\color{blue}\rm Tr}(T)\notin [0,\infty).$ As a consequence of the proof of the three items already proved, we conclude that $T$ is none of the following: parabolic, hyperbolic and elliptic. Therefore, $T$ must be loxodromic.
